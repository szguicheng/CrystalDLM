\subsubsection{Synthetic Polymers}
\label{sec:synthetic-polymers}

Many synthetic polymer samples comprise chains with different lengths, sequences, or branching patterns. A repeat unit specifies local chemistry, but it does not fully specify such a sample. Polymer tokenization therefore depends on whether the input describes an ideal repeating structure, an individual chain, or an ensemble of chains~\citep{lin2019bigsmiles,aldeghi2022ensemble}. Here, 1D refers to string representations and 2D to chemical connectivity graphs, following the sequence and graph methods in Section~\ref{sec:ai}. In both cases, the choice of units determines how repeat chemistry, connections between units, and sample-level information enter the model. Table~\ref{tab:polymer-tokenization} summarizes representative approaches.

\textbf{1D representations.}
For linear polymers, polymer SMILES (PSMILES) describes a repeat unit with two wildcard connection sites, often written as \texttt{[*]}. The wildcards mark backbone continuation rather than the chemical end groups of a finite chain. One difficulty is that the same ideal polymer admits several descriptions: \texttt{[*]CO[*]} and \texttt{[*]COCO[*]}, for example, encode the same periodic connectivity using different repeat lengths. polyBERT addresses this redundancy through polymer-specific canonicalization, including repeat reduction and normalization of the repeat boundary, before applying a 265-token SentencePiece vocabulary~\citep{kuenneth2023polybert}. The resulting sequence represents the canonical PSMILES string. The fingerprint subsequently obtained by averaging token embeddings cannot be uniquely mapped back to that string. Thus, the information retained by the string tokenizer must be distinguished from that retained by the learned fingerprint.

TransPolymer extends the input beyond repeat chemistry by combining SMILES with available descriptors, including component ratios, chain arrangement, molecular weight, and degree of polymerization~\citep{xu2023transpolymer}. Its chemically informed tokenization keeps multi-character elements such as \texttt{Si} together, assigns tokens to discretized numerical values, and uses descriptor-specific markers for missing values. This allows samples with the same repeat chemistry to be distinguished when the relevant descriptors are available. The numerical precision and completeness of the representation depend on how those descriptors are recorded and discretized.

Generative models also need a representation that can be decoded into a polymer structure. polyBART uses pseudo-polymer SELFIES (PSELFIES), obtained by closing and canonicalizing a repeat-unit graph, reopening it, and introducing astatine atoms as proxies for the two connection sites~\citep{savit2025polybart}. SELFIES symbols then provide input units compatible with a pretrained molecular language model. The proxy atoms carry a polymer-specific meaning: polyBART checks that a decoded structure contains exactly two singly bonded At termini. This check is separate from molecular validity, and neither criterion alone establishes that a proposed polymer can be synthesized.

These repeat-unit strings describe deterministic connectivity. BigSMILES instead describes ensembles through stochastic objects containing repeat-unit alternatives and bonding descriptors that specify permitted connections~\citep{lin2019bigsmiles}. Its syntax accommodates statistical copolymers and branched structures without enumerating individual chains. The order of units in a stochastic list does not specify their order along a chain. For tokenization, the meaningful boundaries include repeat units, stochastic objects, and labeled bonding sites; losing the labels or object boundaries changes the permitted connectivity. BigSMILES supplies this representation language, rather than a particular token vocabulary. Its original formulation specifies possible structures without assigning a probability to each realization, so additional statistics are needed to describe a quantitative ensemble.

\textbf{2D representations.}
Chemical graphs expose connectivity directly, with atom or bond attributes as local units and adjacency as explicit relation data. Polymer graphs must also describe connections across repeat boundaries. Aldeghi and Coley represent molecular ensembles using directed edges weighted by connection probabilities, together with the relative abundances of the constituent units~\citep{aldeghi2022ensemble}. Their weighted directed message-passing neural network (wD-MPNN) uses these quantities to distinguish copolymer composition and architecture; average degree of polymerization is additionally supplied in their phase-prediction task. This representation retains an ensemble average, but not its full heterogeneity. The authors identify specific ambiguities: gradient and block copolymers can share the same average connectivity, while samples with the same average chain length can have different dispersities.

GRIN examines a related problem: the embedding of a polymer can change when its graph is constructed with a different number of repeats~\citep{zhu2025grin}. It combines repeat-unit augmentation with max-aggregation graph learning inspired by maximum spanning trees. The augmented graphs share the same property label, so each repeat-count variant is trained against the same target. Its invariance applies to these graph variants; physical chain length remains a separate sample variable. The reported copolymer experiments cover block and alternating architectures; they do not establish generality to arbitrary stochastic sequences. Both GRIN and wD-MPNN learn features over prescribed atom and bond graphs, rather than a discrete vocabulary of polymer motifs.

\textbf{Implications for token design.}
Repeat units or larger motifs offer possible coarser tokens, provided their attachment sites and inter-unit bonds remain specified. A useful polymer representation must be stable under equivalent choices of repeat boundary while retaining differences in composition, sequence statistics, and chain architecture. Molecular-weight distribution and processing or measurement conditions require additional fields when they matter to the task. Reconstructing a repeat-unit string is therefore a narrower objective than reconstructing a sample specification. Comparisons should assess both: whether equivalent descriptions yield consistent representations, and whether chemically or statistically different samples remain distinguishable. Alternative descriptions of the same polymer must also remain within one dataset split, as in the augmentation protocol used by TransPolymer~\citep{xu2023transpolymer}.
